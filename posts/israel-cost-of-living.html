Essay 001 — Economics

Israel is a rich country that is too expensive to live in

On paper, Israel is one of the twenty richest countries on earth. Adjust for what money actually buys, and it drops out of the top forty. That gap is not a statistical curiosity. It is the difference between a comfortable life and a tight one.

David Zeff20 August 202612 min Data: IMF WEO April 2026

Israel produces a great deal of money and very little breathing room. The economy is projected to generate about $720 billion in 2026 — roughly $69,800 for every person in the country. That is a top-twenty figure globally, ahead of Austria, Germany and the United Kingdom.

Now ask anyone doing a weekly shop in Jerusalem whether Israel feels like a top-twenty country. The answer will not match the statistic.

Both things are true at once, and the reconciliation is simple: Israel earns like a rich country and pays like an expensive one. A large share of the wealth this economy creates is consumed by the price of housing, food, transport and the ordinary business of living.

The fifteen-place fall

There are two ways to rank a country's income. The first converts everything to dollars at the market exchange rate. The second asks what that money buys at local prices. Every country moves a little between the two. Israel moves more than any other member of the OECD.

Ranked by market dollars, Israel sits 11th among the 38 OECD countries. Ranked by what those dollars buy, it sits 26th. Nothing about Israel's productivity changed between the two columns. Only the prices did.

What each number is actually measuring

Gross Domestic Product measures the total value of goods and services produced inside a country. Divide it by the population and you get GDP per capita: average output per person, commonly used as a rough proxy for average income.

The trouble is that converting it into US dollars at the market exchange rate tells you nothing about what those dollars buy once they land. Imagine two countries. In one, output per person is $70,000; in the other, $60,000. The first looks richer — until you notice that rent, groceries and transport cost a third more there. The person earning $60,000 may well be living better.

Purchasing Power Parity — PPP — is the correction for this. Instead of using the market exchange rate, it converts currencies using the relative price of a comparable basket of goods. It answers a different and more useful question: not how much money is there, but how much it buys.

A necessary caveat

PPP-adjusted GDP per capita is not take-home pay. It measures the volume of output an economy produces at local prices, spread across its population. Israel's figure of $59,095 does not mean Israelis earn $59,095, and it does not mean they consume less than they do.

What it does mean is precise and worth stating carefully: once international price differences are accounted for, each unit of Israeli economic output commands fewer real goods and services than the market exchange rate implies.

How expensive, exactly

The ratio between a country's nominal and PPP figures is itself a measurement. It is the comparative price level — how costly a country is relative to a benchmark. Setting the United States at 100, Israel comes out at 118.

Only Iceland and Switzerland are more expensive in the entire OECD. Israel is 52 percent above the OECD's simple average, and it is more expensive than Norway, Denmark, the Netherlands and Ireland — countries with visibly higher output per person.

Being expensive is not automatically a scandal. Switzerland is expensive and Swiss output per person is roughly $106,000. Prices tend to rise with productivity, and a rich country with high wages will have costly haircuts and restaurant meals. That much is ordinary economics.

The Israeli case is different, and the difference is the entire argument.

More expensive than its income explains

Plot every OECD country's output per person against its price level and a clear relationship emerges: richer countries are more expensive. Fit a line through it, and you can ask a sharper question — given how much a country produces, how expensive should it be?

Israel sits further above that line than any other country in the OECD. Its output per person predicts a price level of about 73. It runs at 118. That is roughly 62 percent more expensive than its own productivity accounts for.

The OECD's 2025 survey puts the gap at roughly 35% using a different method and vintage. The methods disagree on magnitude and agree on direction.

Israel has high incomes. It also has very high prices — higher than its incomes explain.

Why "raise wages" is only half an answer

The instinctive response to an affordability crisis is to push incomes up. Higher wages are good, and Israel should want them. But wages and prices are not independent. If pay rises while rent, groceries and electricity rise alongside, much of the gain evaporates before it reaches anyone's savings account.

There is a second route to a higher standard of living, and it is chronically underrated: make the same salary buy more.

Consider a household earning ₪12,000 a month. If essentials consume ₪10,000, ₪2,000 remains. Now suppose competition and supply-side reform cut those essential costs by 15 percent. The salary has not moved. Nobody got a raise. But the money left at the end of the month rises from ₪2,000 to ₪3,500 — a 75 percent increase in whatever this household has left to save, invest, or simply not worry about.

This is the mechanism that makes cost-of-living reform worth doing. It is also why it is so rarely done: the beneficiaries are diffuse and the losers are concentrated and organised.

So why is Israel expensive?

There is no single cause, and some of the causes are not anyone's fault. Israel is a small economy, geographically isolated from most of its natural trading partners, with limited overland trade and real security costs. None of that is a policy choice.

But a substantial share is. The OECD's 2025 Economic Survey of Israel identifies barriers to trade, pervasive red tape, weak competitive pressure and restrictive product-market regulation as central contributors to Israeli prices. Those are decisions, and decisions can be reversed.

Seven things Israel can actually do

01Open the country to imports

If Israeli consumers can buy from dozens of international suppliers, Israeli firms have to compete. More choice compresses margins and margins are a large part of the price.

Israel has already started. The What's Good for Europe Is Good for Israel reform took effect on 1 January 2025, creating an import track based on compliance with European regulation rather than duplicate Israeli testing — dozens of EU regulations adopted in phases through 2028. A parallel American track followed, allowing products meeting US federal requirements into the Israeli market as an alternative to certain Israeli standards.

The direction is right. The principle should go further: if a product is legally sold in the United States or the European Union, Israel should not duplicate the testing wherever safety permits. Importing should be boring — fast, cheap and predictable.

02Break up concentrated markets

Competition does not function when consumers face a handful of suppliers. The OECD flags concentration in Israeli food retail specifically, where a small number of firms dominate entire categories.

The fix is usually not price controls. It is arranging matters so that five firms have to compete against fifty. If a foreign supermarket chain wants to enter Israel, that should be easy. If a new importer wants to undercut an incumbent, that should be easy too. Competition is the most durable form of consumer protection there is.

03Build more housing

Housing is the largest single line in most Israeli household budgets and the one that has moved most. When demand grows faster than supply, prices rise — and Israel's population grows at roughly 2 percent a year, unusually fast for the OECD.

Demand-side help alone can backfire. Give buyers more purchasing power while supply stays fixed and a good share of that money capitalises straight into higher prices. What is needed is faster approvals, more rezoning, greater density near transport, infrastructure ahead of new neighbourhoods, and a real rental market. Supply does not make housing cheap overnight. Over a decade it decides whether housing is affordable at all.

04Make transport cheaper

Housing and transport are the same problem viewed twice. When people are priced out of living near work, they pay the difference in cars, fuel, insurance and hours. The objective is not more roads; it is making it cheaper to get where you need to go — which usually means transit, and housing near the jobs.

05Make it easier to start a business

Every unnecessary regulation is a cost. Somebody must interpret it, apply for the permit, host the inspection, wait out the approval. Those costs end up in the price the customer pays.

The OECD's recommendations here are concrete: a one-stop shop for company formation, a "silence is consent" rule for permits and licences, and fewer administrative hurdles overall. The test for any rule should be whether its benefit justifies the cost it imposes.

06Increase competition in financial services

Banking competition shows up in mortgage rates, loan terms, deposit rates, fees and investment products — the machinery of a household's finances. Consumers should be able to switch providers easily and compare prices honestly. A bank whose customer could leave tomorrow has a reason to offer a better deal today.

07Don't confuse subsidies with affordability

A government can always make something cheaper by paying part of the bill. That is not the same as making it cost less. Subsidising an expensive product while shielding its producers from competition means taxpayers fund the inefficiency permanently.

Targeted assistance for people who need it is entirely appropriate. But it should sit alongside removing the barriers that made things expensive, not instead of it.

The honest counterargument

Three objections deserve to be taken seriously, and this argument is stronger for meeting them.

The shekel is doing some of this. A country with a booming export sector tends to have a strong currency, and a strong currency mechanically raises the comparative price level without anyone raising a single price tag. Israeli tech exports have done exactly that. Some of the 118 is the shekel, not the supermarket — and a strong shekel also makes imports cheaper, which cuts the other way.

Some of the cost is structural and not going anywhere. Distance from trading partners, limited overland routes and genuine security expenditure are real. No competition policy erases them.

PPP comparisons are imperfect. The baskets are approximations, the conversion factors are estimates, and reasonable methods disagree — my own calculation puts the gap between Israeli prices and what its output predicts at around 62 percent, while the OECD's puts it near 35 percent. Those are different numbers.

But note what the objections do and do not do. They adjust the magnitude. They do not touch the direction. Every serious method finds Israeli prices high relative to Israeli output, and the OECD — no one's idea of a radical institution — attributes a meaningful share of it to trade barriers, red tape and thin competition. You do not need the number to be 62 rather than 35 for the policy conclusion to hold.

The goal is higher real living standards

The objective is not a bigger GDP figure. Growth matters because it represents real production, but what people care about is what that production lets them do with their lives. A ₪15,000 salary in an affordable country can support a better life than ₪20,000 in an expensive one. That is the whole reason purchasing power is worth measuring.

Israel does not need to become a richer country before Israelis become better off. It needs the wealth it already produces to go further. Open the markets. Ease imports. Dismantle the barriers to competition. Build housing. Improve transport. Cut the bureaucracy. Make it easy for a new company to challenge an old one.

A country can have world-class technology, formidable exports and a $700 billion economy, and still make daily life unnecessarily expensive for the people who live in it. Israel's economic challenge is not only to grow the pie. It is to make the pie cost less to buy.

A paycheck in Israel should buy more.
